Market Intelligence — Ireland 28 AUG 2026

Mapping the EPR landscape: the Republic of Ireland's public and private hospital sector

Ireland enters the second half of 2026 with its most consequential digital health decision in a generation live on eTenders: a national electronic health record for the HSE. Beneath that headline, the underlying picture is fragmented — a private sector that has consolidated almost entirely around MEDITECH Expanse, a public sector still running on legacy patient administration systems and single-purpose national programmes, and one greenfield, Epic-based deployment at the new children's hospital that keeps slipping its opening date. This report maps supplier positions hospital by hospital as a baseline for FHI's Ireland database.

FHI Intelligence | Research & Data Collection Briefing | Updated 28 August 2026 | ~4,100 words

Key findings

  • The HSE published its national EHR tender in March 2026 — "HSE One Health Record" — with bids due 24 March and a phased go-live starting with the Dublin and North East health region in 2029, extending to all six regions by 2032. No supplier has yet been named.
  • MEDITECH Expanse is the dominant platform in Ireland's private hospital sector. Bon Secours Health System (5 hospitals, €25m, 2023), Blackrock Health (4 sites, €25m, 2023), Mater Private Network (branded "Perl", €26m, 2022) and UPMC Ireland (up to 11 facilities, announced June 2025) have all selected Expanse, giving MEDITECH an effective monopoly across the four largest private groups.
  • Epic's only confirmed footprint in the Republic is the new children's hospital (Children's Health Ireland, "Project Ogham", €86m programme approved 2022), and that deployment's live date has moved with the hospital's construction delays — most recently flagged as at risk of slipping to autumn 2027. Epic, however, already runs the whole of Northern Ireland's health and social care system: see the all-island box-out in the Conclusions.
  • Public acute hospitals have no common EPR. Coverage is a patchwork of national single-domain systems — MN-CMS/Cerner Millennium for maternity (6 of c.19 maternity units live), NIMIS/Agfa for imaging (nationwide), and one standalone acute EPR deployment (Kainos Synergy/Evolve at Tallaght University Hospital, 2022) — sitting on top of legacy patient administration systems that this research could not individually attribute by supplier.
  • Bed and staff data below is a mix of current supplier/hospital disclosures and 2019 HSE figures (the last point at which comparable hospital-level bed numbers were published in the sources available to this research); site-level workforce numbers are not routinely published by the HSE and are flagged as a priority for the FOI pipeline.

1. National context: a health system in the middle of two transitions

Two structural changes are running in parallel across Irish healthcare and both bear directly on how EPR/EHR procurement will be organised over the next five years. First, the HSE is mid-transition from seven Hospital Groups (Ireland East, RCSI Hospitals, Dublin Midlands, Saolta, UL Hospitals, South/Southwest, and Children's Health Ireland) to six geographic Health Regions (Dublin and North East, Dublin and Midlands, Dublin and South East, Mid West, South West, and West and North West) [7][9][10][11][12]. Hospital Groups remain the operational and contracting reference for most existing IT relationships at the time of writing, so this report organises hospitals by Hospital Group and notes the Health Region mapping where it changes the procurement picture.

Second, and more consequentially for this database, Ireland is procuring its first national electronic health record. Government approved the HSE's preliminary business case in April 2025 and cleared the procurement to begin on 5 February 2026 [1][2][3]. The formal tender — branded "HSE One Health Record" — was published on eTenders.gov.ie in March 2026 (bids closed 24 March 2026), seeking a single platform to cover an estimated 5.3 million people and 160,000 HSE staff [6]. The published rollout sequence is: Stage 1, HSE Dublin and North East region, live 2029; Stage 2, one further region, 2030; Stages 3–4, the remaining four regions, 2031–2032 [5][6]. No preferred bidder has been named publicly as of this report.

FHI Data

This is the single largest EPR opportunity in the Irish market's history and directly analogous to NHS England's frontier EPR programme in commercial scale, though on a c.4–5 year longer runway. FHI will track the eTenders.gov.ie notice, shortlist and framework award through to Stage 1 go-live as a standing entry in the Ireland database, cross-referenced against the England/Wales/Scotland/NI EPR supplier map already held for the UK.

Alongside the flagship programme, the Department of Health approved a €263m HSE Digital for Care capital plan for 2026, which funds several adjacent, already-live building blocks that any national EHR supplier will need to interoperate with: the National Shared Care Record (€8m in 2026, GP prescription access among the year's releases), the HSE Health App (€7m, 200,000+ downloads), continued expansion of MN-CMS maternity records to three further hospitals, virtual ward capacity, and €20m of ICT spend tied to the new Children's Health Ireland hospital [4]. None of this spend has yet consolidated the public acute sector onto a common EPR — that is what the 2029–2032 programme is for.

2. Public (HSE) hospital sector

Ireland's public acute hospital system comprises roughly 30 acute hospitals of varying scale (HSE's own "Model 2/3/4" classification) plus a long tail of smaller community, orthopaedic, ophthalmic and psychiatric hospitals that fall outside the scope of acute EPR investment and are summarised at the end of this section rather than tabulated individually. No public acute hospital in Ireland currently runs a comprehensive, vendor-supplied EPR spanning all clinical domains; each instead layers national single-domain systems (maternity, imaging, occasionally document management) on top of a legacy patient administration system (PAS) whose specific supplier this research was not able to confirm hospital-by-hospital from public sources — a clear FOI target for the next phase of the database.

2.1 Children's Health Ireland and the new children's hospital: Ireland's largest single EPR deployment

Children's Health Ireland (CHI) — which runs the existing children's hospitals at Crumlin, Temple Street and Tallaght pending consolidation onto a single new-build campus at St James's — represents the most advanced and highest-value EPR programme in the Irish market, and the one most directly comparable to UK EPR frontier-programme scale. Cabinet approval for the EHR business case (part of the wider "Project Ogham" digital programme) was sought in July 2022, with total EHR implementation costs put at €86m against an overall hospital capital cost of €2.2bn [32]. The vendor was selected via an open procurement process run 2019–2021; while the original 2022 press coverage of the approval declined to name the supplier, CHI's own subsequent communications and integration-partner disclosures confirm the core EHR is Epic [33]. InterSystems HealthShare Health Connect was contracted as the integration engine linking Epic to more than 70 other clinical, building-automation, pharmacy and robotics systems on the new site, using HL7v2/FHIR [33]; Telefónica Tech holds a five-year infrastructure/IT services contract, and Ascom supplies alerts/notification integration with Epic Rover [33].

The hospital's opening date has slipped repeatedly — most recently flagged to an Oireachtas committee in March 2026 as at risk of moving from a stated June 2026 target to autumn 2027 [34]. Because the Epic go-live is tied to the physical opening, every construction delay is functionally an EHR programme delay; FHI should track the Public Accounts Committee hearings on this programme as a leading indicator for the go-live date rather than relying on CHI's own announcements alone.

🔍 Supplier Watch — Epic

Epic's only confirmed acute-sector foothold in the Republic of Ireland is CHI's new children's hospital. There is no public evidence of an Epic bid, pilot or reference site in any adult HSE hospital or private group researched for this report. Given Epic's growing UK NHS frontier-programme footprint, CHI's live experience (whenever it lands) will be the reference case Epic uses in any future HSE One Health Record bid — worth monitoring CHI staff/clinician commentary once live for early signal on Epic's positioning for the national tender. Epic's live, complete deployment just across the border in Northern Ireland is arguably a stronger reference still — see the all-island box-out in the Conclusions, below.

2.2 National single-domain systems

MN-CMS (Maternal & Newborn Clinical Management System) is Ireland's most mature clinical EHR programme, running on Cerner Millennium (now marketed as Oracle Health). Phase 1 went live at four sites — Cork University Maternity Hospital, University Hospital Kerry, the Rotunda Hospital and the National Maternity Hospital, Holles Street — completing digitisation by September 2019 [35]. Nenagh Hospital became the fifth site in July 2025, with the HSE's stated intention to extend to the remaining c.14 of Ireland's roughly 19 maternity units in further phases [36][37]. This is by a clear margin the strongest existing evidence base FHI holds for a specific EPR vendor operating inside HSE hospitals and should be the anchor entry for Cerner/Oracle Health's Ireland market-share record.

NIMIS (National Integrated Medical Imaging System) is the nationwide PACS/RIS layer, originally deployed by McKesson (Horizon PACS/RIS) and now under Agfa HealthCare/Change Healthcare stewardship, live across effectively all public acute hospital imaging departments [38][39]. It sits outside the core EPR/EHR scope of this report but is directly relevant to the database as the one genuinely national clinical system already in place — and a template for how a national programme can succeed in Ireland where enterprise EPR has not yet been attempted.

Kainos Synergy/Evolve at Tallaght University Hospital (see table above) remains, as far as this research could establish, a single-site deployment rather than a national rollout, notwithstanding CEO commentary in 2022 about "sharing learnings" with other providers [17]. Kainos separately holds a place on the HSE's Digital Marketplace-style DPS framework for health document management systems (branded Kainos Evolve), which is a distinct, lower-value proposition from the Synergy acute EPR and should not be conflated with it in the database.

3. Private hospital sector

Ireland's private hospital market is far more consolidated on EPR than the public sector, and the pattern is stark: of the four hospital groups researched that have made a public EPR vendor announcement, all four chose MEDITECH Expanse. Ireland's first Expanse deployment was at the Galway Clinic in 2017 [41]; every major group announcement since has followed the same platform.

Market implication: MEDITECH's position across Bon Secours, Blackrock Health, Mater Private and UPMC Ireland means it now covers the large majority of private acute beds in the state. Any EPR vendor targeting the Irish private market without a MEDITECH-competitive offer is effectively locked out of the four largest groups for the length of these contracts (typically multi-year Expanse deployments). The open questions worth tracking are Beacon Hospital's still-unconfirmed EPR programme (the PMO hiring signal is the strongest lead) and St Vincent's Private, both of which remain uncommitted and are the two most plausible near-term competitive-tender opportunities in the private sector.

6. Data gaps, methodology and recommended next steps for the database

This report was built from HSE and eHealth Ireland official pages, gov.ie press releases, sector trade press (Digital Health News, HTN, Pulse+IT, Medical Independent), supplier announcements, and hospital/Wikipedia reference data, gathered in a single research pass. It is a strong first layer for the Ireland database but has three structural gaps that FHI should close before treating any hospital record as complete:

  • Legacy PAS suppliers at public acute hospitals are almost entirely unconfirmed. This is the single biggest gap in the report — roughly 25 of 30 tabulated public acute hospitals have no independently sourced PAS/EPR supplier. This is a natural target for the FOI pipeline (a standard question set to each hospital group/hospital: "name of patient administration system, supplier, contract start/end date, EPR roadmap").
  • Bed numbers are inconsistent in vintage. The most complete comparative bed dataset located was HSE data from August 2019 (via RTÉ); several hospital-level figures used above are more recent but sourced from the hospital's own or Wikipedia's reporting and may not be like-for-like (inpatient-only vs. total incl. day/psychiatric beds). Recommend sourcing the HSE's current National Service Plan bed appendix or a targeted FOI for a single, dated, hospital-by-hospital bed capacity table.
  • Staff numbers are not routinely published at individual hospital level. HSE workforce reporting (Health Services Employment Report, Health Service Personnel Census) is compiled by staff category and, at finest grain located in this research, by hospital group — not by site. Only Beaumont Hospital (~3,000), Beacon Hospital (~1,100) and Sports Surgery Clinic (~500+) had citable site-level staff figures. Recommend an FOI request to each hospital group for site-level whole-time-equivalent (WTE) counts if this field is a required database attribute.

The smaller community, psychiatric and single-specialty public/voluntary hospitals listed in Section 2 (roughly 25 sites) and several of the smaller private hospitals in Section 3 (Kingsbridge, Barringtons, Highfield, St John of God) were not systematically researched for bed/EPR data in this pass; they are lower commercial priority for EPR tracking but should be captured at minimum for completeness (name, location, type, ownership) in the next data-collection cycle.

7. Conclusions

Ireland's national EHR decision will be made on its own merits — clinical fit, cost, delivery risk, interoperability with MN-CMS, NIMIS and the National Shared Care Record — and nothing in this report suggests the outcome is preordained. But one factor sits outside the standard procurement scorecard and is, in FHI's view, significant enough to name explicitly: the Republic would not be choosing an EPR vendor in isolation. Northern Ireland has already made its choice, and made it work.

All-island implications: what an Epic win in the Republic would mean.

Northern Ireland's encompass programme — Epic, contracted in June 2020 for an initial £275m — is not a pilot or a partial deployment. It is complete. All five Health and Social Care Trusts plus the Northern Ireland Ambulance Service went live in sequence (South Eastern HSC Trust, November 2023; Belfast HSC Trust, June 2024; Northern HSC Trust, November 2024; Southern and Western HSC Trusts, both 8 May 2025), giving Epic a single, unified acute, mental health and social care record across a population of 1.9 million [57][58]. The final capital and revenue cost has since risen to c.£400m [57] — a material data point in itself for any Irish value-for-money comparison, on either side of the border.

Were the HSE's "One Health Record" programme to select Epic for the Republic's 5.3 million people, the practical effect would be a single EHR vendor spanning almost the entire island of Ireland — c.7.2 million people across all 32 counties, two jurisdictions, two health systems and two funding models, but one clinical software base. That has no precedent anywhere in these islands (NHS England, Scotland, Wales and the Republic's own hospital groups all run mixed-vendor estates) and would be a distinctive selling point in Epic's HSE bid: a live, complete, next-door reference site is a materially stronger proof point than any UK frontier-programme comparator. It would also be operationally significant in its own right — all-island cancer care (the National Cancer Control Programme already runs cross-border pathways), the cross-border children's cardiac and paediatric surgery arrangements involving CHI, and cross-border patient mobility under the EU/UK trading arrangements would all sit, at least in principle, on a common record architecture for the first time.

FHI's read is that this cuts two ways for the market. It strengthens Epic's competitive position going into the HSE tender beyond what its limited direct Irish footprint (CHI only) would otherwise suggest — the encompass go-live, not Project Ogham, is the reference customers will actually ask about. But it is not a foregone conclusion: MEDITECH's near-total lock on the Irish private sector, and the general HSE preference for competitive, multi-vendor procurement over vendor lock-in, both cut the other way. FHI will track this explicitly as a standing entry in the database — "all-island EPR consolidation risk/opportunity" — through the HSE One Health Record shortlist and award.
